# How we search: follow the receipts

We search to make a better decision. We are looking for what could change our minds, not collecting links to dress up an idea we already like.

Market Intel asks what is happening, for whom and against which alternatives. Segment asks which group deserves our attention and what the obtainable opportunity could be worth. Both can start with notes or a context dump. When browsing is available, both investigate; a context dump is a starting point, not an excuse to skip research.

## Where we look

- **Public filings:** “We check revenue, customer counts, business risks and where companies are placing their bets.” Compare definitions and periods before using their numbers.
- **Patents and academic research:** “We look beneath the marketing for technical approaches, prior art and emerging competition.” A filing shows a technical claim or intent, not adoption or a proven moat.
- **Help-wanted pages:** “We check the capabilities companies are investing in and the problems they’re trying to solve.” A job posting is evidence of hiring intent; strategy is an inference.
- **Census and labor statistics:** “We count the relevant firms, sites and roles before we multiply anything.” Match geography, industry classification, unit and year. People, plants and paying accounts are different denominators.
- **Industry and trade research:** “We compare the official counts with what the industry says about needs, adoption and constraints.” Read the methods; members, survey samples and sponsored reports have limits.
- **Procurement, contracts and channels:** “We follow the buying trail: what was requested, funded and actually awarded?” Announced budget, tender, contract value and revenue are different numbers.
- **Actual offerings and pricing:** “We inspect what companies sell today, to whom, in which units and at what published price.” List price is an anchor, not proof of realized price or willingness to pay.
- **Customers and workarounds:** “We excavate what people already do, including spreadsheets, phone calls and doing nothing.” Vendor testimonials and public reviews are clues with selection bias, not a representative demand study.

Choose sources for the question. We don't search every category to complete a checklist. Use the country's relevant statistics, filings and procurement systems; don't force a US lens onto another market.

## Show the receipts beside the claim

“Every meaningful claim gets a label: actual data, inferred, best guess or unknown.”

“We keep the source, date and limitations attached so you can inspect the receipts.”

For sourced factual claims and numerical inputs, use a nearby clickable citation to the actual inspected document: a descriptive document/table title linked in Markdown to its direct supporting URL. Include publisher, publication/reference date and relevant page/table/row. A source ID alone or a list of homepages at the end does not let a reader check the claim. Supplied-only documents get filenames and locations; unavailable public links stay unavailable.

“A job posting is actual evidence of hiring intent. What it suggests about strategy is inference.” The interpretation cites the posting but keeps its own label. Guessed inputs explain their method; calculated results retain the uncertainty of their inputs.

“Five websites repeating the same announcement don’t become five independent witnesses.” Trace original sources, compare independent channels and expose disagreements. An announcement remains intent until relevant money, hiring, procurement, contracts or actual offerings corroborate it. Corroboration strengthens a hypothesis; it doesn't establish customer demand by itself.

## Which investigator hat we wear

These are teaching metaphors for public-source investigation, not instructions to impersonate anyone or claim credentials.

| Hat | What you can say while showing the search |
|---|---|
| CIA field agent | “Follow public channels, partners and procurement trails to understand how this market actually operates.” |
| Forensic accountant | “Dig through public filings and reconcile the numbers behind market size and competitive reach.” |
| Patent examiner | “Look beneath the marketing language for technical approaches, prior art and crowded territory.” |
| Investigative reporter | “Compare what companies announce with what they’re hiring for, selling and reporting.” |
| Archaeologist | “Excavate legacy tools and workarounds to understand what customers already do.” |
| FBI profiler | “Look for patterns in needs, constraints and buying behavior to shape possible segments, then carry that context into personas, pains and gains.” |

The hats change the question we ask. They don't give us permission to invent motives, stereotype customers or collect private information.

## How we turn searching into a decision

1. State the decision and research capability. Show a three-bullet search plan.
2. Reuse credible supplied evidence; fill consequential gaps and check stale or conflicting claims.
3. Collect and inspect sources before synthesizing. Distinguish the observation from the story we tell about it.
4. Challenge our preferred reading. Empty competitive space might be opportunity, or a dead zone with no demand.
5. For sizing, build from countable units and price assumptions, then reconcile against independent published estimates. Explain scope differences and disagreement; don't average our way to confidence.
6. End with a short readout: what we learned, clickable receipts, what remains uncertain and the next decision. Segment ends with TAM/SAM/SOM populations, potential dollars and reasoning.

Without browsing, say so. Use available documents, label estimates and name the missing evidence. Never invent a citation or call a research plan a finding. Best guess means an honest provisional model, not permission to avoid accessible facts.

Enough to investigate is not automatically enough to invest. Simulation, patents, hiring and market estimates do not establish demand, savings, willingness to pay or a successful product.
