## What this is

A walk through how a product team can make a good decision about an idea before they build it. The starting request is a classic:

> Build an AI predictive-maintenance dashboard.

That sentence is a solution, not a problem. The case study shows ten moves that turn it into a testable bet, stopping for a human decision at each step.

The point: AI made building fast and cheap. It did not make knowing what to build any easier. Build the right thing before building it right.

Learn faster. Decide better. Then build.

## The people and the situation (all fictional)

- **Target (ESTIMATE / BEST GUESS):** maintenance managers in mid-sized manufacturing plants.
- **Situation:** two equipment reports disagree before an investigation decision, and someone has to choose what to check next.
- **Desired outcome:** clearer next-investigation decisions.
- **Evidence:** none. Frequency, consequence, baseline, commercial value and willingness to pay are UNKNOWN.
- **Biggest unanswered question:** is report uncertainty really the obstacle, or does permission and coordination matter more?
- **The brutal truth we are afraid of:** the information may already be good enough, and the real barrier is who can approve and when. A dashboard would not fix that.

## The ten moves

Each move makes a small artifact, labels what is known and unknown, and stops for a human decision. A recommendation is not an approval.

1. **Market Intel:** what is the landscape, and how much of what we know is evidence?
2. **Segment:** which bounded context are we deliberately choosing to learn about? Includes rough TAM, SAM and SOM, all marked as estimates.
3. **Persona:** who is this person, in what situation, trying to make what progress?
4. **Opportunity Solution Tree:** which needs are worth pursuing, and which solutions and cheap experiments hang off them?
5. **Value Prop vs. Differentiation 2x2:** a bake-off of the candidate solutions, a rival and today's workaround. Does the value matter, and is the difference meaningful?
6. **Positioning Statement:** why is this for this person, and why would they choose it over today's workaround?
7. **Solution Hypothesis:** what do we believe will change, and what would make us revise or stop?
8. **Storyboard:** the story in six frames, from who has the problem to who else gets the win.
9. **Minimum Viable Narrative:** the smallest story worth testing.
10. **Prototyping:** what is the smallest, cheapest test we can run to learn the most brutal truth?

Three questions mark the three parts:

- **What problem are we solving, and for whom?** Moves 1 to 3.
- **Where do we play, where do we win?** Moves 4 to 6.
- **What must be true, how do we learn?** Moves 7 to 10.

## Evidence labels

- **INFERRED:** a reasonable interpretation of evidence.
- **ESTIMATE / BEST GUESS:** plausible, not verified.
- **UNKNOWN:** not enough evidence. Unknown is not "low." Never invent a number because a table looks incomplete.

## The small handoff

Every move passes on six things so the next move can start without the whole history:

- Target
- What we believe
- Evidence
- What is inferred
- Desired outcome
- Biggest unanswered question

## Simulation is not evidence

Synthetic personas, scenarios and stories generate hypotheses. They are not customers. These aren't customers. They're hypothesis-generating machines. A finished artifact, a good-looking storyboard or a working prototype does not show anyone wants the thing.

## Try it on your own idea

1. Pick one move, the one closest to the decision you face. Start where the decision is.
2. Bring your own context: who it is for, the outcome you want, and what you know versus guess.
3. Ask for the artifact, keep the evidence labels, and stop at the decision.
4. Carry only the small handoff to the next move.

Each move exists as a repeatable "skill" and as a single paste-ready prompt you can use in any chat, with no install.

## Prompt, Skill, Agent, Plugin

Choose the lightest useful abstraction. A prompt explores. A skill codifies a repeatable play. An agent delegates across steps. A plugin distributes the kit. In this case study a person ran the moves. There is no autonomous discovery agent, and ten finished artifacts do not prove demand.
