## 1. Purpose of This Guide

This guide gives you a realistic business scenario grounded in real industry research: AI predictive maintenance for mid-sized US manufacturing plants. It is your chance to step into a product team's shoes and apply AI-assisted discovery workflows to a decision that matters before anyone builds anything.

The intent is not to critique existing vendors or past decisions. It is to practice structured, evidence-aware thinking in a believable context. You will use prompting, context-building, research and prototyping skills to explore a real opportunity space and produce something useful.

## 2. Use Case Scenario

You are part of a cross-functional product and business team at an industrial software company. Your company already sells software to manufacturers. Your mandate is to explore whether an AI predictive-maintenance product for mid-sized US plants is worth investing in, and if so, what the first, smallest bet should be.

Manufacturing maintenance is under pressure. Skilled maintenance technicians are scarce and the workforce is projected to grow while hiring stays hard. Most plants still run a large share of their maintenance reactively. Equipment data lives in several places: sensors, maintenance records, technician knowledge, spreadsheets. And a long list of vendors now sells predictive maintenance, mostly to large enterprises.

A senior leader, the highest-paid person in the room, just walked in with a solution:

> **"Build an AI-enhanced maintenance dashboard for managers at mid-sized manufacturers."**

You are not going to say no. You are going to explore the idea through discovery and see what holds up. That sentence is a solution, not a problem. Your task is to explore where and how your company could win, whether a real, frequent, costly problem sits behind that request, and what the cheapest honest test would be. You will use generative AI to research the space, shape the opportunity, generate synthetic examples that are clearly labeled as synthetic, and build a proof-of-concept artifact your team can react to.

> Think of yourselves as an internal venture team. You have real industry data, real competitors and a real open question. Your job is to figure out whether to build, and what, before anyone writes a line of code.

### Our Vision

To become the preferred decision-support partner for maintenance teams in mid-sized US plants, helping them make clearer, more defensible calls on what to investigate next, and making the evidence behind each call visible.

### Guiding Principles

- Anchor every decision in plant outcomes: fewer surprises, defensible decisions, less wasted technician time.
- Outcomes over outputs. A dashboard is a solution candidate, not a need.
- Evidence should increase before fidelity increases. Do not build what a conversation could settle.
- Favor opportunities where we can be meaningfully different from the status quo and from incumbents.
- Think about the whole workflow, including planning, permission, trust and capacity, not only the data.
- Label synthetic material as synthetic. Simulation generates hypotheses, not customer truth.

## 5. Market Opportunity

US manufacturing is a large, plant-fragmented industry in the middle of a structural shift. Skilled labor is tight, automation is spreading, and the maintenance function that keeps equipment running is growing in importance while hard to staff.

Some of what the research found:

- There are 284,452 manufacturing plants in the US and 22,643 of them employ 100 to 499 people. Those plants are 8.0 percent of plants but employ 37.2 percent of manufacturing workers (ACTUAL DATA, Census County Business Patterns 2023; the 100 to 499 band is our assumption of "mid-sized").
- About 10,943 of those mid-sized plants sit in six discrete subsectors: fabricated metal, machinery, transportation equipment, plastics and rubber, computer and electronic, and electrical equipment (INFERRED from the same data).
- The US employs about 439,640 industrial machinery mechanics, about 53 percent of them in manufacturing. BLS projects the mechanic, maintenance worker and millwright group to grow 14 percent from 2025 to 2035 (ACTUAL DATA).
- Deloitte and the Manufacturing Institute project about 3.8 million manufacturing jobs to fill from 2024 to 2033, with about 1.9 million possibly unfilled (ACTUAL DATA, projection).

### Common Plant Challenges

All of the following are hypotheses supported by the sources noted. None is a verified finding about mid-sized US plants.

- **Reactive maintenance remains large, and nobody agrees by how much.** Surveys put it anywhere from about a third to well over half of work, depending on who asked and how. The sources are mostly vendor-sponsored and not comparable. The government guide most often cited is from 2010.
- **Time to act is scarce.** In one vendor-sponsored survey, staffing and resources (31 percent) and scheduling conflicts with production (31 percent) were the top barriers to preventive work.
- **Equipment information is scattered.** Paper and spreadsheets are still common in some surveys (one UK and Ireland survey found 59 percent paper and 37 percent Excel). No US mid-sized plant measure was found. UNKNOWN.
- **Predictive tools ask for trust before they have earned it.** Peer-reviewed interview studies say early false or missed alarms are expected, failure data is scarce, and people lose confidence in systems faster than in people after an error. Those studies are small and mostly European.
- **The cost of downtime is quoted often and measured rarely.** The widely cited figures come from large global firms and vendor-sponsored reports. No source measures it for mid-sized US plants. UNKNOWN.
- **Analyst market-size numbers disagree.** The 2025 global predictive-maintenance market is estimated at $9.2B to $14.1B, and 2031 forecasts differ by about 3.5 times, with no method disclosed. Do not use any as a market size.

> **Curiosity Prompt**
> What do you still not know about this market that could change your direction entirely? Name at least two unknowns your team would need to resolve before committing to an offering design.
>
> **AI Workflow:** Use your AI assistant with the case file documents to research one of your unknowns. Compare what the AI surfaces with what your team already knows from experience. Ask it to show where each claim came from and flag anything it cannot source.

## 6. Segments to Consider

Not all plants face the same problems with the same urgency. Consider where a new offering could be meaningfully different. Plant counts are from Census 2023 (ACTUAL DATA). Everything about their needs is a hypothesis.

### Mid-Sized Discrete Plants (100 to 499 employees)

About 22,643 plants, with 10,943 in six discrete subsectors. Large enough to have a maintenance function and equipment that costs real money when it stops, small enough that they may not have a dedicated reliability team or enterprise software. Vendors mostly target either large enterprises or very small teams, so the middle may be under-served. INFERRED. Who buys and with what budget is UNKNOWN.

### Larger Plants (500 or more employees)

About 3,384 plants (2,347 at 500 to 999 and 1,037 at 1,000 or more). Most of the enterprise predictive-maintenance vendors show customers here. More engineering depth, more integration work, longer sales cycles. Well served by incumbents with strong references. INFERRED.

### Smaller Plants (50 to 99 employees)

About 21,365 plants. Maintenance may be one or two people, often with paper or spreadsheets. Public per-user software pricing from maintenance-software vendors is aimed here (ACTUAL DATA: $20 to $75 per user per month tiers). Likely price sensitive and thin on planning capacity. INFERRED.

> **Curiosity Prompt**

## 7. Competitors and Alternatives

The instinct when looking at this market is to compare against other predictive-maintenance vendors. That instinct leads in the wrong direction.

The real question for a plant is not which vendor to pick. It is whether to add another system at all, or keep relying on what already works well enough: a technician's judgment, the maintenance history and a spreadsheet. That is the frame that matters.

Vendor descriptions below are vendor claims from their own sites unless noted. "Our angle" is a hypothesis, not a finding.

| Competitor | Why chosen | Pain points | Wins in | Our angle (hypothesis) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Maintenance software** (Fiix, UpKeep, MaintainX) | Public pricing, mobile work orders, easy start. Fiix sits in Rockwell. Autodesk announced an agreement to buy MaintainX. | A system of record, not a decision aid. Does not claim to resolve conflicting information. Limble's own product chief says a CMMS alone does not reach predictive maintenance without data discipline. | Teams getting off paper and spreadsheets | Complement the record with a clearer basis for the call |
| **Predictive platforms** (Augury, Siemens Senseye, Samotics) | Sensors plus AI, ranked and explained alerts, strong funding and references (Augury raised $75M at a valuation over $1B in 2025) | Quote-only pricing, enterprise and process-industry skew, no vendor page we read says "mid-sized plant" | Large plants with budget and engineers | Meet mid-sized plants without enterprise sales weight |
| **Sensor plus software bundles** (Tractian) | Hardware, diagnostics and a work-order app in one | Claims are vendor claims. No accuracy figures or independent proof on the pages we read. | Plants willing to instrument assets | Be explicit about evidence and uncertainty |
| **The status quo** (technician judgment, maintenance history, spreadsheets) | Trusted, free, already inside the workflow. Many plants reach high availability through experienced people. | Knowledge is undocumented and people retire. The reasoning behind a call is invisible to others. | Most plants today | Make the reasoning visible without replacing the person |
| **In-house tools and OEM service contracts** | Control, vendor-tied expertise | Costly to build and maintain; prevalence UNKNOWN | Plants with large internal teams | UNKNOWN |

> **Curiosity Prompt**
> If you were a maintenance manager at a 250-person plant, what would you need to see in a proof of concept to take a new tool seriously, as opposed to trusting your technician and your own records?

## 9. Design Reference: The Decision View

No published interface specification exists for this concept, so there is nothing to copy. That is the point. The dashboard idea in the original request is not invented from nothing, but it is a solution looking for a problem. The design reference for this case is the decision itself.

Your task is to describe, not prescribe. Choose what a maintenance manager would need to see at the moment two reports disagree, and why. You are not writing code or picking a layout. You are describing the situation, the actor, the information exchanged and the consequence. Rendering tools can propose a layout later, and you can critique it.

Useful references for how existing products present alerts and priorities, all from the vendors' own public pages (vendor claims, not evidence of effectiveness): Augury's ranked alerts with cause, urgency and recommended action; Siemens Senseye's framing of alert overload; Fiix and MaintainX work-order views.

> **Descriptive prompt for a builder (adapt, do not copy):** A maintenance manager has two conflicting reports before a shift handover. Show where each report came from, how current it is and how sure it is, so the manager can pick a next investigation and explain why. Descriptive, not prescriptive. Use synthetic data labeled SYNTHETIC. Do not include real equipment commands.

> **Curiosity Prompt**
> Before you look at any product, write down what a maintenance manager would need to see when two reports disagree. Then compare your list to how existing tools present alerts. Where did the real design surprise you?
>
> **AI Workflow:** Ask your AI assistant to turn your list into a six-frame storyboard: who has the problem, what the problem is, the oh crap moment, the solution arrives, the solution aha moment, and sharing the love. Ask it to keep every claim about benefit labeled as a story hypothesis.

## 10. Summary of the Challenge

Your company already has many of the pieces needed: software customers in manufacturing, trusted relationships and engineering capacity. The industry data says there is a large, tight, fragmented field of mid-sized plants, and a crowded vendor landscape that mostly serves bigger buyers or smaller teams.

The challenge is not to invent a new technology stack. The challenge is to find out whether a real, frequent and costly problem exists, who owns it and who pays, and what the smallest test is that would tell you. Then to combine what exists into an offering concrete enough for a real maintenance manager to react to before anyone commits to building it.

Across the exercise you will move through three questions, using generative AI to speed each one while keeping a person in charge of every decision.

### Part 1: What problem are we solving, and for whom?

- **Market Intel:** build an evidence-aware view of the landscape before narrowing, with a source register.
- **Segment:** choose a bounded group of plants deliberately, with plant counts and labeled estimates for total, serviceable and obtainable.
- **Persona:** describe the person in the situation, with jobs, pains, gains and a problem framing statement. Quotes and demographics stay UNKNOWN without research.

### Part 2: Where do we play, where do we win?

- **Opportunity Solution Tree:** keep the outcome explicit, then opportunities, solution candidates and cheap experiments.
- **Value versus differentiation bake-off:** place each candidate, the incumbents and today's workaround on one map.
- **Positioning statement:** make the proposition explicit, with the reason to believe UNKNOWN until tested.

### Part 3: What must be true, how do we learn?

- **Solution hypothesis:** if, for, then we will, two tiny acts of discovery, one quantitative and one qualitative measure, and a rule for when to revise or stop.
- **Storyboard:** the story in six frames.
- **Minimum viable narrative:** the smallest story worth testing, with the action and response loop running three to six exchanges.
- **Prototyping:** the smallest, cheapest test that returns the most brutal truth, or just enough signal to pivot, punt or pursue.

By the end, your team will have a sourced view of the industry, a chosen segment, a hypothesis-grade persona, a map of options, a testable hypothesis, a storyboard and a plan for the first tiny act of discovery. Nothing is built, and nothing is validated until real people say so.

The goal is not a finished product. The goal is something a maintenance manager, a planner or a plant manager could look at and say:

*"Now I understand what you mean." "That part feels real." "That part worries me." "Here is what I would need to see next."*

That feedback is the win.

> **Where the demo landed (one possible finding, not the answer)**
> In the live demo, the chain moved from the dashboard request to something different: **intervention by prediction instead of intervention by exception**. That means acting before a failure instead of reacting to an alarm or a breakdown. Whether that is delightful, hard to copy and margin-enhancing is a hypothesis. Incumbents already sell prediction, pricing is quote-only and willingness to pay is UNKNOWN. Run your own version and compare.
