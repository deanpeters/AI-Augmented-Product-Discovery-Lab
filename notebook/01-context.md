## 2. Audience and outcome

**Audience:** Product Managers, founders and product teams deciding what deserves to be built. Many are early stage, short on runway, and under pressure to ship something because AI makes shipping easy.

**Outcome for the audience:** Watch product judgment reduce uncertainty before fidelity goes up. Leave with one play they can try on their own problem the next morning.

**Takeaway line:** Learn faster. Decide better. Then build.

## 3. The core argument

AI made building dramatically cheaper and faster. It did not make knowing what to build any easier.

That raises the cost of poor judgment. Teams can now build the wrong thing faster, and burn runway on a polished version of a weak assumption.

So the scarce skill is not building. It is deciding what is worth building, and being honest about how much you actually know when you decide.

The line Dean uses on himself: AI can help you build the wrong thing really fucking fast.

## 4. Beliefs the show must reinforce

These are the product management beliefs underneath everything. Each one should be felt in the live work, not just stated.

- **Outcomes over outputs.** A feature list is not a strategy.
- **Context is work.** Good AI output starts with someone doing the work of supplying context.
- **Discovery precedes commitment.** Evidence should increase before fidelity increases.
- **Progress means uncertainty reduced.** Activity is not progress.
- **A prototype is an experiment,** not an early production system.
- **Simulation generates hypotheses, not customer truth.** The spoken guardrail: "These aren't customers. They're hypothesis-generating machines."
- **Product Managers are not Jira clerks,** backlog administrators or delivery coordinators. Frameworks exist to improve thinking, not to create bureaucracy.

## 5. What the room should watch happen

The audience watches product thinking happen, live, in a conversation with an AI assistant. The pattern repeats ten times:

1. A human supplies context and picks a mode.
2. The assistant produces an editable artifact.
3. The artifact labels what is known, inferred, guessed and unknown.
4. The assistant stops at a human decision. A recommendation is not approval.
5. Only a small handoff moves to the next motion, not the whole transcript.

The point is not that AI did the work. The point is that a person stayed in charge of every decision that mattered.

## 6. The ten motions (macro to micro)

A spoken Hall of Shame opens the show. Then ten discovery motions run in this order.

| # | Motion | The question it answers | Artifact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Market Intel | What is the landscape, and how much of what we know is evidence? | Market Intelligence Brief |
| 2 | Segment | Which bounded context are we deliberately choosing to learn about? Includes speculative sizing: TAM from population data, SAM from trade and industry data, SOM from competition plus reach and capacity. All sizes are ESTIMATE until sourced. | Segment Selection Brief |
| 3 | Persona | Who is this person, in what situation, trying to make what progress? Follows the Productside framing canvas in three steps: (1) list pains, gains and jobs-to-be-done around the persona, (2) pick the top pain, gain and job, (3) write the Problem Framing Statement: I am, trying to, but, because, which makes me feel. Then the Productside proto-persona canvas: name, portrait, bio and demographics, quotes, desired outcomes and goals, needs and pains. Demographics and quotes stay UNKNOWN without research, and nothing is invented. Also captures workaround and stakes. | Situational Persona |
| 4 | Opportunity Solution Tree | Which needs are worth pursuing, and which solutions and cheap experiments hang off them? | Opportunity Solution Tree |
| 5 | Value Prop vs. Differentiation 2x2 | A bake-off of the solutions from the Opportunity Solution Tree, with competitors and the current workaround on the same map. Where does each sit: does the value matter, and is the difference meaningful against the real alternative? Two separate questions. | 2x2 |
| 6 | Positioning Statement | Why is this for this person, and why would they choose it over today's workaround? Follows the Productside positioning statement canvas: For (target customer), who (statement of opportunity), the (product name) is a (category) that (key benefit). Unlike (primary competitive alternative), our product gives (primary differentiation). | Positioning Statement |
| 7 | Solution Hypothesis | What do we believe will change, and what would make us revise or stop? Follows the Productside solution hypothesis canvas: If we (an action or solution on behalf of the target persona) for (the target persona) then we will (the desired outcome or job-to-be-done); 2 tiny acts of discovery (we will test our assumptions by); 1 quantitative and 1 qualitative metric (we will know it is valid if within a stated number of days or weeks we observe). Numbers and timeframes stay bracketed or UNKNOWN until a person sets them before the test. | Solution Hypothesis |
| 8 | Storyboard | What is the story? Six frames: who has the problem, what the problem is, the "oh crap" moment when it comes to a head, the solution arrives, the solution aha moment (the person with the problem uses it), and sharing the love (they help others enjoy the same success). Follows the Productside storyboard canvas: a solution summary (if we do this, we expect that) over six frames, one sentence each. Aim for authenticity, simplicity and emotion. The product is the hero of the story. | Storyboard |
| 9 | Minimum Viable Narrative | What is the smallest story worth testing? Five parts: Setup (who they are, what they need), Encounter (what the system shows them), then a loop of Action (what they do or give) and Response (what the system does with it) repeated 3 to 6 times, then Resolution (what success feels like). The person acts, the system responds, the payoff comes at the end. Follows the Productside MVN canvas: carry in the prototype hypothesis, name the target audience in one sentence, write the five beats in one or two sentences each, then assemble a descriptive, not prescriptive, prompt for a no or low code builder. A prototype is not a product, it is a story: the shortest version that still lets you observe the behavior. | Minimum Viable Narrative |
| 10 | Prototyping | What is the smallest, cheapest test we can run to learn the most brutal truth? Includes experiment status and the next evidence decision. | Prototype Experiment Brief |

Notes on the order, because people get it wrong:

- Market Intel discovers the landscape. Segment is a separate, deliberate choice.
- Persona carries jobs, pains and gains. Opportunities live in the tree, not in the persona.
- Storyboard comes before Minimum Viable Narrative. The MVN tightens the storyboard.
- There is no agent-strategy canvas, no actor map, and no eleventh "learning review" stage.
- The chain is loosely coupled on purpose. If one demo fails, the next one still runs from a small handoff.

### The small handoff

Every motion passes on six things:

- Target
- What we believe
- Evidence
- What is inferred
- Desired outcome
- Biggest unanswered question

Plus whatever artifact the next motion actually needs.

## 7. Evidence labels

Used on every claim, on every artifact, and visible on slides when relevant.

- **ACTUAL DATA:** directly supported by a source, with a URL and a date.
- **INFERRED:** a reasonable interpretation of evidence.
- **ESTIMATE / BEST GUESS:** plausible, not verified.
- **UNKNOWN:** not enough evidence. Unknown is not "low". Never invent a number because a table looks incomplete.

## 8. The demo scenario (SYNTHETIC)

The running example is Industrial IoT and manufacturing maintenance.

- **Starting request, a classic premature solution:** "Build an AI predictive-maintenance dashboard."
- **Target (best guess):** maintenance managers in mid-sized manufacturing plants.
- **Desired outcome:** clearer decisions about which conflicting equipment concern to investigate next.
- **Biggest unanswered question:** is report uncertainty actually the obstacle, or do permission and coordination matter more?
- **Evidence:** none. No interviews, no plant observations. Everything is synthetic and says so on screen.

The scenario is chosen because dashboards, copilots, alerts and predictive platforms are exactly where teams jump too early. At each step the show asks: what problem are we assuming this solves, what outcome are we pursuing, and what remains true if this solution disappears?

Where the demo lands: a tiny, cheap test (a conversation, a storyboard or a wireframe) can answer the learning question before anyone builds anything. The Prototyping question is: what is the smallest, cheapest test we can run to learn the most brutal truth? The tiniest act of discovery wins. Lo-fi wins. We are not attached to any one medium, including paper. Jeff Patton: "The most expensive way to test your idea is to build production-quality software."

## 9. Premature solutionism: the thing to catch

Watch for early jumps into dashboards, copilots, chatbots, agents, apps, alerts, integrations, recommendation engines, automation and predictive-maintenance platforms. The show models the discipline of asking, "What is the need, and what outcome do we want?" before "What do we build?"

## 10. Fidelity rule

Do not raise fidelity just because AI makes it cheap. Before raising it, ask: what can we learn at this fidelity that we could not learn more cheaply? A polished prototype is not stronger evidence. It may just be a more expensive rendering of weak assumptions.

## 11. Prompt, Skill, Agent, Plugin

A short closing idea, not a discovery stage. Choose the lightest useful abstraction.

- **Prompt:** explore. Paste it into any chat.
- **Skill:** codify. A bounded, repeatable play.
- **Agent:** delegate. An operator that pursues an outcome across steps.
- **Plugin:** distribute. The kit.

Honest status: Dean operates the chain today. No autonomous discovery operator is built. Ten completed artifacts do not prove customer demand.

Each motion exists both as a skill and as a self-contained paste-ready prompt, so attendees can try a play in a normal chat with no install and no repo access.

## 12. Honest limits (say them out loud)

- The scenario is fictional. No customers were interviewed.
- Generated artifacts are hypotheses, not evidence.
- A working prototype is implementation evidence, not validated demand.
- Audience reactions in the room are not plant evidence.
- AI demos obey Murphy's Law. Every live motion has a saved fallback. Recovery line: "AI demos obey Murphy's Law, so I brought receipts."
